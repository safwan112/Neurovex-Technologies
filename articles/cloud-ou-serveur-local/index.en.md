Where should the company's files be stored? Where should the management software, email, and accounting run? For a long time, the answer was obvious: a server in a back room, maintained by an IT provider. Today, the cloud offers an attractive alternative: no hardware to buy, access from anywhere, automatic updates.

Yet the choice isn't as simple as it looks. Each option has its strengths, its limits, and its hidden costs. The right answer depends less on technology than on your business, the quality of your internet connection, your regulatory constraints, and your internal resources. This article helps you see clearly and ask the right questions before deciding.

## What exactly are we talking about?

An **on-premises server** is a computer installed in your offices that hosts your files and some applications. You buy it, you own it, and you are responsible for keeping it running, securing it, and replacing it.

The **cloud** refers to services hosted in a provider's data centers and accessed over the internet. There are several forms:

- **online applications**, such as business email, collaborative office suites, or management software accessed through a browser;
- **online storage**, to share and sync files;
- **virtual servers**, rented on demand, on which you can install your own software.

Between the two, a **hybrid setup** combines cloud services and on-site equipment, each used for what it does best.

## Cloud strengths

The cloud has become the norm in many SMEs for good reasons:

- **Access from anywhere**: employees work in the office, at a customer's site, on the road, or at home with the same tools.
- **No hardware investment**: no server to buy or replace every five to seven years.
- **Updates and baseline security handled by the provider**: patches, power redundancy, cooling, and data center monitoring.
- **Flexibility**: users can be added or removed in a few clicks, and storage capacity grows with your needs.
- **Easier collaboration**: several people can work on the same document at the same time.

For a growing company with several sites or mobile teams, these advantages are often decisive.

## Cloud limits

The cloud comes with constraints too:

- **Dependence on the internet**: without a connection, accessing your tools becomes difficult or impossible.
- **Recurring costs**: the monthly subscription looks modest, but it adds up year after year and grows with the number of users.
- **Data location**: data may be hosted abroad, which can raise questions for certain activities or contracts.
- **Provider lock-in**: switching providers can be long and complex, especially with large volumes of data.
- **Shared security**: the provider secures its infrastructure, but managing accounts, passwords, and access rights remains your responsibility.

This last point is often misunderstood. An account without two-factor authentication and with a weak password remains vulnerable, whatever the security level of the data center.

## On-premises strengths

An on-site server still has strong arguments in certain situations:

- **High local performance**: for handling large files (plans, videos, high-resolution images), a local network is much faster than an internet connection.
- **Working without internet**: teams keep working even during an outage.
- **Full control**: you know exactly where your data is and who has physical access to it.
- **Compatibility with older software**: some business applications have no cloud version.
- **Predictable long-term cost**: once the hardware is bought, recurring costs are limited to maintenance and power.

## On-premises limits

In return, an on-premises server demands constant effort:

- **Upfront investment**: server, disks, UPS, licenses, installation.
- **Maintenance**: system updates, disk monitoring, replacing faulty parts.
- **Physical and logical security**: a locked room, cooling, protection against power cuts, firewall, antivirus.
- **Backups**: without an off-site copy, a fire, theft, or ransomware attack can wipe everything out.
- **Replacement**: after five to seven years, the hardware must be renewed, with a migration to organize.
- **Remote access**: it's possible, but it must be configured carefully, for example through a VPN, so as not to open security holes.

## Hybrid: the best of both worlds?

Many SMEs choose a mixed model that fits the reality of their business. A common setup looks like this:

- **email and office suite** in the cloud, for mobile access and collaboration;
- **large files or specialized software** on a local server or network storage, for speed;
- an **off-site backup** in the cloud, to protect the local server's data against disasters.

This approach lets you move forward gradually: you start by migrating what brings the most benefit, without disrupting what already works well. It does require good coordination so that access rights, backups, and security stay consistent across both environments.

## The internet connection question

For a company that relies on the cloud, the internet connection becomes critical infrastructure, just like electricity. Before migrating, assess:

- the available **bandwidth**, for downloads but above all for uploads, which is often overlooked even though it determines how fast files sync;
- the **stability** of the connection and how often it drops;
- whether you have a **backup connection**, for example a second line from another operator or 4G/5G access, with a router that can switch over automatically.

A half-day outage with no fallback can cost far more than a second line. For a company that is fully in the cloud, this redundancy is not a luxury.

## Security and backups: leave nothing to chance

Whichever model you choose, security rests on a few shared principles:

- accounts protected by two-factor authentication;
- access rights granted according to each employee's real needs;
- up-to-date, protected workstations;
- regular backups, with at least one copy outside your premises and one copy protected against modification or deletion;
- regular restore tests.

One point deserves emphasis: the cloud is not a backup. If a file is deleted or encrypted by ransomware in a synced folder, the change can spread everywhere. Cloud services often provide a recycle bin and version history, but for a limited time. An independent backup is still recommended.

## Data and regulations

Some data requires particular care: customers' personal data, health data, documents covered by professional secrecy, information subject to contractual clauses. In Morocco, Law 09-08 governs the processing of personal data, and some transfers abroad may require formalities with the CNDP.

Before choosing a cloud service, identify the data involved, check where it will be hosted, and seek legal advice if needed. For some activities, keeping the most sensitive data on a local server or with a hosting provider in Morocco can simplify compliance.

## Comparing costs over five years

Comparing a monthly subscription with a hardware purchase only makes sense over the same period. Calculate the total cost over five years:

**For an on-premises server**: hardware purchase, licenses, installation, UPS, off-site backup, maintenance contract, power, internal management time, and a provision for replacement.

**For the cloud**: subscriptions multiplied by the number of users and by sixty months, extra storage, a stronger and backup internet connection, the initial migration, and account administration time.

The result is often surprising, in one direction or the other. For a small team without large files, the cloud is usually cheaper. For an organization handling large volumes of data with a stable number of users, an on-premises server can remain competitive.

## How to plan a successful migration

Whether you move to the cloud, renew a server, or build a hybrid setup, a migration touches every employee's daily work. Careful planning avoids most of the pain.

1. **Take inventory.** List your applications, file volumes, users, and the dependencies between systems. You can't migrate what you don't know you have.
2. **Clean up first.** Delete obsolete files, close unused accounts, and reorganize folders. Migrating clutter only makes it more expensive to store.
3. **Define access rights.** Take the opportunity to decide who needs access to what, instead of copying old permissions that nobody understands anymore.
4. **Test with a pilot group.** Migrate one department first, gather feedback, and fix problems before the general rollout.
5. **Plan the switchover.** Choose a quiet period, such as a weekend, keep the old system available in read-only mode for a while, and make sure a verified backup exists before anything is deleted.
6. **Train and support users.** A short training session and a clear point of contact for questions during the first weeks make adoption much smoother.

A migration is also a good moment to review security settings, enable two-factor authentication everywhere, and document the new architecture.

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing on price alone.** The cheapest monthly plan can become expensive if it lacks the features, storage, or support you need.
- **Forgetting the upload bandwidth.** Syncing large files to the cloud over a weak connection frustrates everyone.
- **Believing the cloud replaces backups.** Keep an independent copy of your important data.
- **Keeping an ageing server "until it breaks".** An unplanned failure always costs more than a planned replacement.
- **Neglecting documentation.** Without a clear record of accounts, settings, and providers, every incident takes longer to resolve.

## What about AI?

Artificial intelligence adds a new dimension to this choice. Most AI assistants integrated into office suites and business software run in the cloud, which is one more reason many companies move their email and documents there. Conversely, organizations that want to use AI on highly sensitive data sometimes install models on their own servers so the data never leaves the building. Before deciding, consider which AI features you are likely to use in the coming years and what infrastructure they require.

## A practical example

An architecture firm in Marrakech has twelve employees. Its plans and 3D renderings are large files, and the team often travels to construction sites.

After analysis, the firm opts for a hybrid solution. Email, calendars, and administrative documents move to the cloud, accessible from phones and tablets on site. Current projects stay on fast network storage in the office, synced every night to an off-site backup. A second internet connection ensures continuity during outages. The firm gains mobility without slowing down work on large files.

A year later, the firm reviews the setup. Architects appreciate being able to consult documents and answer emails from construction sites, and the nightly backup has already proved useful when a project folder was accidentally deleted and restored the same morning. The firm also notices that finished projects rarely need fast access, so it decides to move completed projects to cheaper cloud archive storage, freeing up space on the local system and postponing the purchase of new disks. This kind of regular review is what makes a hybrid architecture work well over time: each type of data ends up in the place that suits it best, and the setup keeps evolving with the business instead of being frozen at the day it was installed.

## Questions to ask before deciding

1. What are our main uses, and how large are our files?
2. Do our employees work remotely or across several sites?
3. Is our internet connection reliable, and do we have a fallback?
4. Which data is sensitive, and where must it be hosted?
5. Who handles day-to-day maintenance and security?
6. What is the total five-year cost of each option?
7. Do our business applications exist in a cloud version?
8. How would we roll back or switch providers if needed?

## In short

There is no universal answer. The cloud brings mobility, flexibility, and simplicity; an on-premises server offers performance, control, and independence from the internet; a hybrid setup combines the two. The right choice is the one that fits your uses, your connection, your constraints, and your long-term budget, with serious security and backups in every case.
