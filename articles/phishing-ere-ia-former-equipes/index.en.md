For years, the advice for spotting a fraudulent email was simple: watch out for spelling mistakes, clumsy phrasing, and blurry logos. That advice is now outdated. Thanks to artificial intelligence, fraudsters write flawless messages in perfect French or English, tailored to the industry and even to the style of the person they are impersonating. Some go further and reproduce an executive's voice over the phone.

For SMEs, the risk is considerable. A single click on a malicious link can open the door to account theft, ransomware, or a fraudulent transfer. Small organizations are all the more exposed because they rarely have a dedicated security team. But this is not inevitable: with simple rules, the right tools, and regular training, your employees can become your best line of defense.

## What AI changes for fraudsters

Phishing means tricking someone into revealing information, clicking a link, opening an attachment, or making a payment. AI doesn't change the principle, but it makes attacks more effective in several ways:

- **Writing**: messages are fluent, error-free, and can be produced in large numbers in several languages.
- **Personalization**: using public information (websites, professional social networks, press releases), fraudsters mention real names, projects, or events to make the message credible.
- **Impersonation**: it is possible to imitate someone's writing style, and even their voice from a few recordings available online.
- **Conversation**: some fraudsters use AI to keep up a believable exchange over several messages until they get what they want.

The consequence: you can no longer rely on the form of a message to judge whether it is genuine. You have to look at the context and the request.

AI also lowers the cost of attacks. Campaigns that once required time and language skills can now be launched quickly by less experienced fraudsters, in many languages at once. For a company working in French, English, and Arabic, this means attacks can arrive in any of those languages with the same level of polish. Small businesses, which fraudsters used to consider less profitable targets, are now reached just as easily as large ones. This is why awareness can no longer be limited to a few people: everyone who has an email address or a work phone is a potential target.

## The most common forms of attack

**The fake login email.** A message imitates your email provider, your bank, or an online service and asks you to sign in again through a link. The page looks like the real one, but it captures your username and password.

**CEO or supplier fraud.** A message that seems to come from an executive asks for an urgent, confidential transfer. Or a "supplier" announces a change of bank details for upcoming invoices. This type of fraud can cost very large sums.

**The fake invoice or malicious attachment.** A document presented as an invoice, delivery note, or résumé contains malware.

**Messages on phones.** Fraudulent SMS and WhatsApp messages are multiplying: fake parcels waiting, fake account problems, fake offers. On a small screen, it is harder to check a link's address.

**The phone call with a cloned voice.** A call appears to come from an executive or a known partner and asks for urgent action. The voice is convincing, sometimes generated by AI.

**The malicious QR code.** A QR code in an email, on a poster, or in a document leads to a fake site. It bypasses some filters because the link isn't directly visible.

## The new warning signs

Since style is no longer enough, teach your teams to spot signals linked to context:

- **Unusual urgency**: "to be handled before noon," "your account will be blocked in one hour."
- **A request for secrecy**: "don't tell anyone for now."
- **A request for money or a change of bank details.**
- **A request for credentials** or for a code received by SMS.
- **An unusual channel**: an executive suddenly writing from a personal address or on WhatsApp.
- **An unexpected link or attachment**, even from a known contact.
- **A slightly different sender address**, with one changed letter or a lookalike domain.

A single one of these signals is a reason to stop and check. Several signals together should trigger an immediate alert.

## The golden rule: verify through a second channel

The most effective measure against fraud is also the simplest: **every sensitive request must be confirmed through a different channel from the one it arrived on**.

In practice:

- a transfer request received by email is confirmed by calling the requester's usual number, not the number given in the email;
- a supplier's change of bank details is verified with a known contact, by phone, before anything is modified;
- an urgent call from an executive asking for an unusual action is confirmed by calling them back on their known number or checking with another colleague.

For this rule to work, it must be official and backed by management. An employee must be able to tell their own director "I'll check" without fear of offending them. Some companies even agree on a code word for urgent phone requests.

## Essential technical protections

Training alone isn't enough. A few technical measures greatly reduce the risk:

- **Two-factor authentication** on email and key services: even if a password is stolen, the account stays protected. The strongest methods, such as security keys or authenticator apps, hold up better than SMS codes.
- **Email filtering**, which blocks a large share of fraudulent messages before they arrive.
- **SPF, DKIM, and DMARC records** configured for your domain name, which make it harder for fraudsters to spoof your address.
- **A password manager**, which won't auto-fill credentials on a fake site.
- **Up-to-date devices and browsers**, to limit the exploitation of known vulnerabilities.
- **Regular, protected backups**, so data can be restored after ransomware.
- **Dual approval for payments** above a certain amount.

These measures don't require a large budget, but they must be properly configured and monitored.

## Train regularly, and differently

A yearly half-day training session, forgotten three weeks later, isn't enough. The most effective formats are short, frequent, and concrete:

- sessions of fifteen to thirty minutes, several times a year;
- real examples tailored to each team's work: accounting doesn't receive the same attacks as the sales team;
- demonstrations: showing how closely a fake site can resemble the original leaves a lasting impression;
- short reminders by email or in team meetings when a new scam is circulating.

Also tailor training to the most exposed roles: people who handle payments, executive assistants, HR managers, and anyone whose name appears publicly on the company website.

## Limit what attackers can learn about you

Personalized attacks feed on public information. The more fraudsters know about your organization, the more believable their messages become. Without hiding from your customers, you can reduce what you give away:

- avoid publishing the full organization chart with direct email addresses and roles on your website; a general contact address is often enough;
- ask employees to be careful about what they share on professional social networks, such as details of upcoming payments, supplier names, or travel dates of executives;
- be cautious with automatic "out of office" replies sent to external senders, which can reveal who is away and who is standing in;
- remove old accounts and aliases that are no longer used but still receive email.

Executives deserve particular attention. Their voices appear in videos and interviews, their names on the website, their schedules sometimes on social media. It is worth agreeing with them on a clear rule: they will never ask for an urgent transfer by message or phone without the usual validation process, so any such request is automatically suspicious.

## Phishing simulations

Simulations involve sending employees realistic but harmless fake phishing emails to measure how they react. They show who clicks, who reports, and how vigilance changes over time.

To make them useful, follow a few principles:

- announce the program in advance, without giving dates;
- never ridicule someone who clicked: the goal is learning, not punishment;
- give a short, immediate explanation to those who were caught;
- track the reporting rate above all, which measures the right reaction, not just the click rate.

## Make reporting easy

An employee with a doubt should be able to report it in seconds: a button in the email client, a dedicated address, or a named contact. And someone who clicked should be able to say so without fear.

This is essential. The faster the alert, the easier it is to limit the damage: change a password, lock an account, notify the bank, isolate a computer. A culture where people hide their mistakes for fear of blame turns a minor incident into a crisis.

## What to do after a click

Prepare a simple procedure that everyone knows:

1. don't panic, and don't switch off the computer unless instructed to;
2. disconnect the device from the network if a suspicious attachment was opened;
3. notify the IT contact immediately;
4. change the password of the affected account from another device, if credentials were entered;
5. contact the bank without delay in case of a fraudulent transfer, because quick action increases the chances of blocking the funds;
6. keep the messages involved for analysis.

## Measure progress

To know whether your efforts work, follow a few simple indicators over time:

1. the reporting rate during simulations, which should rise;
2. the click rate during simulations, which should fall;
3. the time between receiving a suspicious message and reporting it;
4. the number of real suspicious messages reported by employees;
5. the number of incidents and their consequences.

Share the results with the teams, focusing on collective progress rather than individual scores. Seeing the reporting rate improve is motivating, and it reminds everyone that security is a shared effort.

## A training calendar for your first quarter

If you are starting from scratch, here is a realistic plan for the first three months:

- **Month 1:** enable two-factor authentication on email, set up a reporting address, and hold a thirty-minute session on the new warning signs and the second-channel rule.
- **Month 2:** check the SPF, DKIM, and DMARC records of your domain, introduce dual approval for payments, and run a first simulation.
- **Month 3:** review the simulation results with the teams, hold a targeted session for payment and HR staff, and schedule short reminders for the rest of the year.

## A practical example

The accountant at an SME in Marrakech receives an email from a regular supplier. The tone is friendly, the signature correct, and the email thread looks genuine. The supplier announces a change of bank and attaches new bank details for upcoming invoices.

A few months earlier, the company adopted a clear rule: any change of bank details must be confirmed by phone with a known contact. The accountant therefore calls their usual contact, who never sent that email. The supplier's mailbox had been hacked. Thanks to a simple rule, the company avoids a fraudulent transfer and warns its supplier, who can in turn secure its account.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Relying on vigilance alone.** Even the best-trained people can slip up on a tired day: technical protections are essential.
- **Training only once.** Techniques evolve, and reminders must keep up.
- **Punishing mistakes.** It pushes people to hide them.
- **Exempting management.** Executives are often the most targeted.
- **Forgetting phones.** WhatsApp and SMS are now major attack vectors.

## In short

AI makes phishing attempts more convincing than ever, but good defenses remain within reach. Teach your teams to judge context rather than style, require second-channel verification for every sensitive request, enable two-factor authentication, train regularly, and make reporting easy. A company where everyone dares to say "I'll check" is a much harder company to fool.
