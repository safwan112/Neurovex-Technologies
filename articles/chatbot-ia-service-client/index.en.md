Your customers contact you at all hours: on your website, by email, on WhatsApp, on social media. They want to know your opening hours, track an order, get a quote, or report a breakdown. And they expect a quick answer. For an SME, keeping up with that pace with a small team is a real challenge.

AI-powered chatbots promise to answer instantly, day and night, in several languages. The promise is real, but it only comes true with good preparation. A poorly designed chatbot gives vague answers, makes up information, or goes around in circles, and the customer leaves more frustrated than before. This article explains, step by step, how to prepare a chatbot that is genuinely useful.

## Rule-based chatbot or AI chatbot: what's the difference?

There are two main families of chatbots. **Rule-based chatbots** follow predefined scripts: the customer clicks buttons or types keywords, and the bot answers according to a decision tree. They are predictable but rigid: as soon as a question falls outside the script, they can't answer.

**AI chatbots** understand natural language. Customers can phrase their question however they like, in their own words and sometimes with typos, and the tool grasps the meaning. Recent versions rely on generative AI and write fluent answers based on the company's documents.

That flexibility has a downside: an AI chatbot can produce a plausible but wrong answer if it doesn't have the right information. All the preparation therefore consists of giving it reliable content and clear limits. In practice, many SMEs combine both approaches: guided flows for simple requests and AI to understand open-ended questions.

## Step 1: define a clear scope

The first mistake is wanting a chatbot that can do everything. Instead, start by analyzing the requests you actually receive. Go through the emails, messages, and team notes from the past few weeks, and sort the questions by topic.

In most SMEs, around ten topics make up the bulk of requests:

- opening hours, address, and contact details;
- delivery or service call lead times;
- order or ticket tracking;
- prices and terms and conditions;
- booking appointments;
- what to do in case of a breakdown or complaint.

Choose these topics as your starting scope. A chatbot that answers ten frequent questions perfectly provides enormous value. For everything else, it must be able to say it doesn't know and point the customer to a human agent.

## Step 2: prepare reliable content

An AI chatbot is never better than the documents it relies on. Before choosing a tool, gather and update your content:

- a clear FAQ, with one question and one answer per topic;
- your terms and conditions of sale and service;
- sheets for your products and services, with up-to-date information;
- your opening hours, including exceptional closures;
- the procedures to follow when something goes wrong.

Write this content in plain language, as if you were answering a customer on the phone. Avoid ambiguous wording, contradictory information across documents, and outdated prices. If a price or lead time depends on many factors, say so clearly and invite the customer to request a quote rather than letting the AI estimate an amount.

Finally, appoint someone responsible for this content. Every change in price, service, or opening hours must be reflected in the chatbot's knowledge base. Without that discipline, the tool quickly becomes a source of errors.

## Step 3: set the tone and the limits

Your chatbot speaks on behalf of your company. Define its tone: formal or informal address, warm or more businesslike style, length of answers. Also specify which languages it should reply in, for example French, English, and Arabic.

Then set explicit limits. The chatbot must not:

- promise a lead time, discount, or goodwill gesture it isn't authorized to grant;
- give legal, medical, or financial advice;
- ask for sensitive information such as a password or a card number;
- answer questions outside the company's scope.

It is also recommended to tell customers clearly that they are talking to an automated assistant. Transparency builds trust and avoids misunderstandings.

## Step 4: plan the human handoff

This is the most important element, and the one most often neglected. A good chatbot recognizes its limits and hands over. Customers must be able to reach an agent easily, without having to type "talk to a person" three times.

Plan several handoff situations:

- the customer explicitly asks for it;
- the chatbot can't find a reliable answer;
- the request involves a complaint, a dispute, or a sensitive topic;
- the customer expresses dissatisfaction.

When handing over, pass the conversation history to the agent. Nothing annoys a customer more than having to explain everything again. Outside opening hours, the chatbot can record the request, say when the team will get back to the customer, and create a follow-up ticket.

## Step 5: choose your channels

Where do your customers contact you? A chatbot on your website is a good start, but in Morocco a large share of conversations happen on WhatsApp. Other customers prefer social media or the phone.

Several tools let you connect the same assistant to different channels. The advantage is keeping a single knowledge base and a single history for each customer. Also think about integration with your existing tools: ticketing software, a calendar for bookings, an order tracking system. The more reliable, up-to-date information the chatbot can access, the more useful it becomes.

For phone calls, smart switchboard solutions can also route calls, answer simple questions, and pass the rest to the right person. Here again, the key is the quality of the content and how easily customers can reach a human.

## Step 6: protect customer data

A chatbot collects information: names, phone numbers, addresses, details of an order or a breakdown. This data must be protected.

Check with the provider where conversations are stored, how long they are kept, and whether they are used to train AI models. Limit the information you ask for to what is strictly necessary. Tell customers how their data is used, for example with a short message and a link to your privacy policy. Finally, restrict access to conversation history to the people who need it.

## Prepare the team and redefine roles

A chatbot changes the work of the team that answers customers. Simple questions largely disappear, and agents receive more complex requests, complaints, and special cases. That's good news, because their expertise is put to better use, but it requires preparation.

Explain to the team what the chatbot's role is and what it won't do. Define who handles handed-over requests, within what timeframe, and with which priorities. Also plan for someone to read conversations and improve the content: this task naturally falls to the people who know customers' questions best.

Agents are also best placed to spot clumsy or incomplete answers. Set up a simple way for them to report these, for example a dedicated channel in your internal messaging tool. Every report becomes an opportunity to improve the tool. Over time, the team comes to see the chatbot as a colleague who filters repetitive requests, not as a threat. Some companies even ask agents to suggest new FAQ entries each week, based on the questions they handled themselves. This keeps the knowledge base close to reality and gives the team a sense of ownership over the tool.

## What budget should you plan for?

The cost of a chatbot depends on several factors: the tool you choose, the number of conversations, the channels you connect, and the configuration work. Many solutions work on a monthly subscription, sometimes priced according to the volume of messages handled. Connecting to WhatsApp Business or to ticketing software may bring additional fees.

Beyond the subscription, don't forget internal time: writing content, testing, regularly reading conversations, and updating. This is often the largest cost, and the one that most determines the quality of the result.

To assess whether it pays off, compare these costs with the time your team currently spends on repetitive questions, and with the requests you lose for lack of a quick answer, especially in the evening and at weekends. A trial of a few months on a limited scope lets you decide based on real figures rather than promises. Keep the trial simple: one channel, a handful of topics, and a clear list of indicators agreed in advance. If the results are good, you will have both the evidence and the experience needed to expand confidently.

## Test before you launch

Before opening the chatbot to the public, test it internally. Ask several colleagues to ask questions the way real customers would, including badly worded, off-topic, or tricky questions. Note incorrect answers, hesitations, and cases where the handoff to an agent doesn't trigger.

A useful test is to prepare a list of about fifty real questions taken from past conversations and check every answer. Fix the content, adjust the instructions, and start again. This work takes a few days, but it prevents unpleasant surprises at launch.

Then launch gradually: first on one page of your website or during certain hours, and expand once the results are satisfactory.

## Measure and improve continuously

A chatbot is never finished. Regularly track a few indicators:

1. the share of questions resolved without human intervention;
2. the handoff rate to an agent;
3. questions left unanswered;
4. customer satisfaction, for example through a rating at the end of the conversation;
5. the team's average response time on handed-over requests.

Read a sample of conversations every week. Unanswered questions show which content to add. Frequent handoffs on the same topic may reveal missing information or a process that needs simplifying. This improvement loop makes all the difference between a gadget and a real customer service tool.

## A practical example

Imagine a Marrakech company that sells and maintains professional photocopiers. Every day, its team receives many WhatsApp messages: toner requests, paper jam reports, questions about service call lead times.

The company starts by sorting three months of messages. It finds that most requests concern five topics. It writes clear answers for each one, with simple procedures for minor issues, and sets up a chatbot that replies on WhatsApp and on its website.

For any blocking breakdown, the chatbot collects the device model, the address, and a description of the problem, creates a ticket, and alerts the on-call technician. The customer receives a confirmation with a tracking number. Technicians spend less time on the phone and arrive at the customer's site with the right information.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Launching without up-to-date content.** The AI will repeat the errors in your documents.
- **Hiding access to a human agent.** A customer stuck with a bot becomes an unhappy customer.
- **Letting the chatbot improvise prices or promises.** Strictly control commercial topics.
- **Never reading the conversations.** Without follow-up, problems go unnoticed.
- **Forgetting other languages.** If your customers write in Arabic or English, test those languages too.

## In short

An AI chatbot can transform your customer service: instant answers, constant availability, and a team relieved of repetitive questions. But its success depends on serious preparation: a clear scope, reliable content, explicit limits, an easy human handoff, and regular follow-up. Well designed, it doesn't replace your team; it lets them focus on the requests that really matter.

If you are unsure where to begin, start with the simplest version: a website assistant that answers your ten most frequent questions and passes everything else to a person. Run it for a month, read the conversations, and improve the content every week. You will quickly see which topics customers care about most, which channels they prefer, and where automation truly helps. From there, adding WhatsApp, appointment booking, or ticket creation becomes a natural next step rather than a risky leap.
