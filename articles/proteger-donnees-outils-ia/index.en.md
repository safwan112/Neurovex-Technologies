A salesperson pastes a contract into an AI assistant to get a summary. An accountant uploads an invoice export to prepare an analysis. A technician shares a screenshot containing IP addresses and credentials to understand an error message. Each of these actions comes from a good intention: saving time. Yet each one can expose confidential information about the company or its customers.

Generative AI has become part of many employees' daily routine, often before the company has defined a single rule. This gap creates a real risk, but it doesn't justify banning these tools. A total ban is rarely respected and deprives the company of significant gains. The right approach is to set a framework for how they are used. This article explains how.

## Why data shared with AI raises questions

When you type text or upload a file into an AI tool, that information is sent to the provider's servers to be processed. Depending on the tool, the version used, and the settings chosen, several things can happen:

- conversations may be **retained** for a longer or shorter period;
- they may be **reviewed** by the provider, for example to detect abuse;
- they may be used to **train or improve** models, which means elements could theoretically influence future answers;
- they may be stored in **another country**, under different legal rules.

Free consumer plans are generally the least protective. Business plans usually include stricter commitments: no training on your data, limited retention, administrative control. But these guarantees vary from one provider to another and change over time. You need to check them, not assume them.

## The main risks for an SME

For a small or medium-sized business, the concrete risks fall into several categories:

- **Leaks of commercial information**: prices, margins, strategy, tender responses.
- **Exposure of personal data**: names, phone numbers, addresses, and histories of customers or employees. In Morocco, Law 09-08 governs the protection of personal data, and the CNDP oversees its enforcement.
- **Disclosure of technical secrets**: network configurations, passwords, source code, infrastructure diagrams.
- **Breach of contractual commitments**: many contracts include confidentiality clauses that forbid passing certain information to third parties.
- **Loss of trust**: a customer who learns their data was shared carelessly may reconsider the relationship.

On top of these comes the risk of wrong answers. A badly summarized contract or an invented figure can lead to a poor decision. Protecting data and checking results go hand in hand.

## Rule 1: prefer business plans

The first measure is to give teams a tool approved by the company. If employees have no official tool, they will use personal accounts, with no control at all.

Before choosing, check the contractual terms for:

- whether your data is used to train models, and whether you can opt out;
- how long conversations and files are retained;
- where the data is hosted;
- who can access it at the provider, and under what circumstances;
- whether the tool offers an admin console to manage accounts;
- which security certifications the provider holds.

Keep a record of these checks. They will be useful when a customer or an auditor asks questions.

## Rule 2: classify information

Not all documents are equally sensitive. A simple classification helps employees quickly decide what they can share. Three levels are often enough:

- **Public**: content that is already published or meant to be, such as website pages, brochures, or job offers. It can be used freely.
- **Internal**: procedures, meeting notes, document templates. These can be used in the approved business tool.
- **Sensitive**: personal data about customers and employees, detailed financial information, contracts, technical secrets, passwords. These must not be entered into an AI tool, unless a solution has been specifically approved for that purpose.

Support this classification with concrete examples specific to your business. "A quote sent to a customer: internal. The full customer file with phone numbers: sensitive." Examples speak far louder than definitions.

Add one simple rule for borderline cases: when in doubt, treat the information as sensitive and ask. It is better to lose five minutes checking than to spend weeks managing the consequences of a leak. To make classification visible, some companies add a short label in document headers or folder names, such as "Internal" or "Confidential". This small habit helps employees make the right decision without thinking twice, and it also clarifies things for new colleagues who don't yet know which documents matter most. Review the classification once a year, because a document that was internal can become sensitive when it starts to include customer data.

## Rule 3: anonymize before sharing

In many cases, you can get help from AI without passing on sensitive information. You simply replace it before sharing the content:

- replace names with "Customer A" or "Supplier B";
- remove phone numbers, addresses, and identifiers;
- round or change amounts when they aren't essential to the question;
- hide IP addresses, server names, and passwords in screenshots.

For example, to improve the wording of a delicate email to a customer, there is no need to mention their name or the exact amount in dispute. To analyze sales trends, a file without customer names is often enough.

## Rule 4: manage access

AI accounts should be managed like the company's other tools:

- use business accounts tied to the company email address, never personal accounts;
- enable two-factor authentication;
- assign licenses by name and keep an up-to-date list;
- when an employee leaves, remove their access the same day and check what happens to their conversation history;
- limit connections between the AI tool and your other applications (email, storage, CRM) to what is necessary.

This last point becomes important with assistants that can read your files and emails. An assistant connected to the whole storage space can surface a confidential document to someone who shouldn't have seen it, simply because sharing permissions were too broad. Before enabling these features, clean up your permissions.

## A special case: connected assistants and AI agents

A new generation of tools goes further than a chat window. These assistants connect directly to your mailbox, calendar, file storage, or business software. Some, often called AI agents, can even take actions: send an email, create a document, update a customer record, or book a meeting.

These capabilities are powerful, but they change the nature of the risk. The question is no longer only "what information do I paste into the tool?" but also "what can the tool see and do on my behalf?" A few precautions are essential:

- connect only the sources the assistant really needs for a given use;
- prefer read-only access whenever possible;
- require human confirmation before any action that sends information outside the company or modifies important data;
- be wary of instructions hidden in documents or emails the assistant reads, which may try to make it do something you never asked for;
- regularly review which connections are active, and remove those that are no longer used.

Treat an AI agent like a new employee with access to your systems: give it only the permissions it needs, supervise its work, and review its access over time.

## Cloud AI or AI hosted on your own servers?

For most SMEs, business cloud services offer the best balance between capability, cost, and security. However, some organizations handle data so sensitive that they prefer to keep everything on their own infrastructure: healthcare providers, law firms, or companies bound by strict contractual obligations.

Open models that can run on a local server or a private cloud now exist. They keep data inside your environment, but they require suitable hardware, technical skills for installation and updates, and generally offer somewhat lower performance than the most advanced cloud services. A hybrid approach is often sensible: a cloud tool for everyday internal and public content, and a local solution reserved for the most sensitive documents.

## Rule 5: train your teams

Most leaks don't come from an attack, but from well-intentioned use. Awareness is therefore the most effective measure.

A short session of thirty to sixty minutes is enough to cover the essentials:

- how AI tools work and where the data goes;
- which tools are allowed and with which accounts;
- what information must never be shared;
- how to anonymize content;
- why answers must always be checked;
- whom to contact in case of doubt or error.

Repeat these messages regularly, for example when a new employee joins or a new tool is adopted. A concrete reminder, with an example from the company's own life, has more impact than a long rulebook.

## What to do when a mistake happens

Despite precautions, mistakes happen: a customer file shared in an unapproved tool, a password pasted into a conversation. How you react then matters as much as prevention.

Plan a simple procedure:

1. the employee reports the incident immediately to a designated contact, without fear of sanction for an honest mistake;
2. the conversation is deleted when the tool allows it;
3. any exposed passwords or keys are changed right away;
4. the contact assesses the data involved and decides what to do, including informing the people concerned or the authorities if regulations require it;
5. the incident is analyzed to prevent it from happening again.

A culture where mistakes are reported quickly protects the company far better than one where they are hidden.

## A practical example

An IT services agency in Marrakech discovers that several technicians use free AI assistants to analyze customers' error logs. These logs contain server names, IP addresses, and sometimes credentials.

Rather than banning AI, management chooses a business tool with contractual guarantees, creates accounts for each technician, and organizes a one-hour training session. It writes a simple rule: before sharing a log, replace customer names, IP addresses, and credentials with generic values. A small internal script even masks this information automatically.

A few months later, technicians still save time on their diagnoses, and the company can answer its customers' questions about confidentiality with confidence.

## Talking about AI with your customers

More and more customers ask their suppliers how they use AI, especially in tenders and contracts. Rather than treating this as a constraint, see it as an opportunity to demonstrate your professionalism.

Prepare a short, clear answer: which tools you use, for which purposes, what guarantees apply to data, and what rules your teams follow. Specify that customer data is not entered into AI tools without appropriate safeguards and that all AI-assisted work is reviewed by a person. If a customer forbids any use of AI on their files, record that requirement and make sure the teams working for them know it.

This transparency reassures customers and sets you apart from competitors who haven't thought about the question. It also forces you to keep your own practices consistent, because what you promise must match what your teams actually do.

## Your checklist

- [ ] A business AI tool is available to teams.
- [ ] The data usage terms have been checked and documented.
- [ ] Everyone knows a simple information classification.
- [ ] Employees know how to anonymize content.
- [ ] Accounts are named, protected by two-factor authentication, and removed when people leave.
- [ ] AI connections to other tools are limited.
- [ ] An incident reporting procedure exists.
- [ ] The rules are regularly repeated.

## In short

AI can bring a lot to an SME, as long as you don't blindly hand it the company's most valuable information. Choose business tools, classify your information, anonymize what you can, manage access rigorously, and train your teams. These simple measures let you take full advantage of AI while preserving your customers' trust.
