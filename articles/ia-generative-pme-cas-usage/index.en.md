In a very short time, generative AI has gone from technological curiosity to everyday work tool. Yet in many small and medium-sized businesses, it is still used in an improvised way: one employee tries an assistant to rephrase an email, another uses it for a translation, with no shared method and no clear goal. The result is real but scattered gains, and sometimes risks that nobody anticipated.

The good news is that you don't need a major transformation project to benefit from these tools. An SME can get visible results within a few weeks, provided it starts with simple, frequent use cases that are easy to check. This article presents five practical use cases, how to set them up, which mistakes to avoid, and a method for measuring what AI actually brings you.

## What generative AI can do, and what it can't

Before choosing use cases, it helps to understand what these tools really do. A generative AI assistant produces text, tables, code, or images from an instruction, usually called a prompt. It is excellent at rephrasing, summarizing, structuring, suggesting alternatives, and speeding up the first draft of a piece of work.

On the other hand, it doesn't "know" things the way a database does. It can invent a figure, a reference, or a contract clause with great confidence. It doesn't know your customers, your prices, or your internal procedures unless you provide them. And it does not replace the judgment of a professional who knows their trade.

The resulting rule is simple: **AI prepares, people validate**. Every use case in this article follows that principle. The tool saves time on the mechanical part of the work, and your teams remain responsible for the final result.

## Use case 1: write faster without sacrificing quality

Writing is the most natural starting point. An SME writes far more than it thinks: customer emails, replies to quotes, product sheets, job offers, social media posts, internal notes, procedures.

AI is particularly useful to:

- produce a first draft from a few key points;
- adapt the same message to several audiences (customer, partner, employee);
- suggest several ways to phrase a delicate sentence, for example when announcing a delay;
- fix spelling and harmonize the tone of a document written by several people.

The gain doesn't come from publishing a text as is, but from skipping the blank page. A salesperson who used to spend twenty minutes writing a follow-up can now spend five minutes reviewing and adjusting a draft. Over a week, the difference becomes significant.

To get good results, give context: who the text is for, what the goal is, which tone to use, how long it should be. A prompt such as "Write a short, friendly follow-up to a customer who hasn't replied to the IT maintenance quote we sent ten days ago" will produce something far more useful than "Write a follow-up email."

## Use case 2: summarize long documents

Meeting notes, contracts, specifications, tenders, technical reports: long documents pile up and take time to read. AI can extract the essentials in seconds.

Some typical uses:

- summarize a tender by listing requirements, deadlines, and selection criteria;
- turn raw meeting notes into structured minutes with decisions and action items;
- compare two versions of a document and flag the important differences;
- prepare a one-page summary for a manager who doesn't have time to read everything.

One precaution is essential: a summary can leave out a crucial detail or misread a clause. For binding documents, such as a contract or a tender response, the summary guides your reading; it doesn't replace it. Always check amounts, dates, and obligations in the original document.

## Use case 3: support internal help

In many companies, the same questions come up again and again: how to connect to the VPN, how to submit an expense report, where to find the quote template, whom to contact about a printer problem. Each question takes a few minutes of a skilled person's time, and those minutes add up.

AI can help in two ways. First, to **write a knowledge base**: from existing procedures, emails, or notes, it produces clear, consistent help articles. Second, some tools let employees **ask questions directly to that knowledge base** and get an answer in plain language, with a link to the relevant procedure.

For this use case to work, the quality of your documents matters more than the tool. An outdated procedure produces an outdated answer. Appoint someone to keep the knowledge base up to date, and encourage teams to report incorrect answers.

## Use case 4: prepare simple analyses

Every SME has data: a sales file, a service call log, a customer request tracker, an accounting export. But few have the time or skills to analyze it regularly.

AI can act as an analysis assistant. From a spreadsheet, it can:

- spot products or services whose sales are growing or declining;
- group customer requests by theme to identify recurring problems;
- suggest spreadsheet formulas or suitable charts;
- write a plain-language commentary on the month's figures.

The point is to speed up reading the data, not to delegate the decision. A trend detected by AI should always be compared with what you know on the ground: a drop in sales may be explained by a stock shortage, a season, or a data entry error. Before sharing files, remove personal and sensitive data, especially if the tool is not a business version.

## Use case 5: translate and adapt content

For a company based in Marrakech, working in several languages is often a necessity: French-speaking customers, English-speaking partners, Arabic-speaking teams. AI has made huge progress in translation and can produce very good versions.

It is useful for translating product sheets, terms and conditions to be reviewed by a lawyer, posts, user guides, or replies to customers. It also helps adapt a text rather than translate it word for word: a sales message that works in French won't necessarily have the same impact in English.

The rule stays the same: have important texts reviewed by someone fluent in the target language. Errors in tone or technical vocabulary are rare, but they happen, and they stand out more on a website or an official document.

## Choosing the right tool

The AI tool market moves very fast, and it is tempting to pile up subscriptions. For an SME, a few criteria are enough to make a sensible choice.

- **Integration with your existing tools.** An assistant built into your email or office suite will be used far more than yet another website to open.
- **Data guarantees.** Check whether your content can be used to train models, where it is stored, and how long it is kept. Business plans usually offer stronger guarantees.
- **Account administration.** Being able to create and remove access centrally is essential as soon as several people use the tool.
- **Quality in French, English, and Arabic.** Test the tool on your own documents before committing.
- **Total cost.** A per-user subscription looks modest, but it multiplies quickly. Reserve licenses for the roles that genuinely benefit.

It is often wise to start with a single tool for the whole company. Good practices are easier to share, training is simpler, and security is easier to manage.

## A practical example: a service SME in Marrakech

Take a fifteen-person company that installs and maintains office equipment. Its manager notices that the team spends a lot of time on administrative tasks and decides to test generative AI for two months.

The manager picks three uses: writing follow-ups on quotes, service call reports, and answering internal questions about procedures. For each one, they appoint a lead and records the time spent before the test.

After a few weeks, the sales team uses the assistant to prepare their follow-ups and saves time every day. Technicians dictate their notes on their phones, and AI turns them into clean reports that are sent to the customer after review. The third use works less well: the internal procedures were too old, and so were the AI's answers. The company therefore decides to update its procedures first.

This kind of review is valuable: it shows what works, what needs preparation, and where to invest next.

## The most common mistakes

First AI projects rarely fail because of the technology. They fail more often for organizational reasons. Here are the mistakes we see most often:

- **Publishing without reviewing.** Generated text may contain false information, the wrong tone, or a promise the company can't keep.
- **Sharing sensitive data in consumer tools.** Customer files, financial data, or contracts should not be pasted into a tool whose terms of use you don't know.
- **Trying to automate everything at once.** Two well-chosen use cases that succeed are better than ten launched without follow-up.
- **Not training the teams.** Writing a good prompt, checking an answer, and recognizing the tool's limits are skills that can be learned. A short training session prevents a lot of disappointment.
- **Forgetting to measure.** Without a starting point, there is no way to know whether AI really saves time.

## Bringing your teams on board

The arrival of AI often triggers two opposite reactions: enthusiasm from those who want to try everything, and concern from those who worry about their job or the quality of their work. Both reactions deserve to be taken seriously.

Explain the goal clearly: freeing up time on repetitive tasks so people can spend more of it on customers, on quality, and on work that requires expertise. Involve employees in choosing the use cases, because they know better than anyone which daily tasks are tedious. Share successes too: an effective prompt found by one colleague can help the whole team.

Finally, leave room for mistakes. The first weeks are about learning. A use case that doesn't work is not a failure if it helps everyone understand what the tool can and cannot do. Some teams find it useful to hold a short fifteen-minute review each week, where everyone shares one thing that worked and one thing that didn't. These small rituals build confidence and spread good habits much faster than a long written guide.

## How to measure the gains

To find out whether AI is worth it, choose a few simple indicators before you start:

1. the average time spent on the task;
2. the number of tasks handled per week;
3. perceived quality, for example the number of corrections needed or customer feedback;
4. the satisfaction of the employees using the tool.

Compare these indicators after four to eight weeks. If the gain is real, extend the use to more people. If it is small, look for the cause: a poorly written prompt, incomplete source documents, an unsuitable tool, or a task that simply doesn't lend itself to AI.

## Your four-week starter plan

Here is a simple method to go from idea to concrete results:

- **Week 1:** list each department's repetitive tasks and pick two use cases that are frequent and easy to check.
- **Week 2:** choose a suitable tool, preferably a business plan, write a few usage rules, and train a small pilot group.
- **Week 3:** test in real conditions and record successes, failures, and time saved.
- **Week 4:** review with the pilot group, adjust the prompts, and decide which uses to extend.

This gradual approach limits risk, reassures teams, and delivers measurable results before you commit to larger investments.

## In short

Generative AI is neither a magic wand nor a gadget. It is a powerful tool for speeding up writing, summarizing, internal support, analysis, and translation, as long as it is used methodically. Start small, always keep a human review, protect your data, and measure the results. SMEs that take this approach gain a real advantage without disrupting their organization.
