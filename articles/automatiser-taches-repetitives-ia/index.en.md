Copying invoice details into a spreadsheet. Sorting the shared inbox. Chasing customers who haven't paid. Preparing the same report every Monday morning. These tasks don't require any special expertise, but they take up a large share of working time, and they are a frequent source of data entry errors and fatigue.

Automation isn't new: spreadsheet macros and email rules have been around for a long time. What artificial intelligence changes is the ability to handle unstructured information: reading a PDF, understanding a freely written email, extracting an amount from a photo of a receipt. Tasks that seemed impossible to automate a few years ago are now within reach of SMEs.

You still need to choose the right tasks and proceed methodically. This article offers a practical approach to automating without risk while staying in control.

## Classic automation and AI-powered automation

It helps to distinguish two levels. **Classic automation** follows fixed rules: "when a form is submitted, send a confirmation email and add a row to the spreadsheet." It is reliable, predictable, and inexpensive, but it only works if data always arrives in the same format.

**AI-powered automation** adds the ability to understand. The tool can read an invoice whose layout varies from one supplier to another, classify an email by its intent, summarize a customer request, or write a personalized reply. In return, the result isn't always one hundred percent accurate, and a check remains necessary.

In practice, the best solutions combine both: simple rules to chain the steps together, and AI only where content needs interpreting. This combination keeps errors and costs down.

## Step 1: spot good candidates

Not every task lends itself to automation. A good candidate usually has several characteristics:

- it is **frequent**: daily or weekly, with enough volume for the gain to be visible;
- it follows **stable rules**: the same steps, in the same order, with few exceptions;
- it relies on **digital data**: emails, files, forms, rather than paper;
- an error has **limited consequences** and can be corrected easily.

To find them, simply ask your teams: "What do you redo every week that feels mechanical?" The answers are often very concrete. For each task, note how long it takes, how often it happens, and how many people are involved. You'll end up with a clear list of priorities.

## Examples of tasks to automate

Here are tasks found in many SMEs, grouped by department.

**Administration and accounting**

- extracting information from supplier invoices (date, amount, VAT, number) into a spreadsheet or accounting software;
- matching payments received against issued invoices;
- sending automatic, polite reminders for overdue invoices.

**Sales**

- recording requests received through forms or email in a tracking file;
- preparing a first draft of a quote from a template;
- scheduling follow-ups on quotes that received no reply.

**Customer service and support**

- sorting incoming emails by topic and urgency;
- automatically creating a ticket when a customer reports a breakdown;
- sending an acknowledgment with an estimated handling time.

**Human resources**

- centralizing leave requests and notifying the manager;
- preparing onboarding documents for a new employee;
- sending reminders for deadlines such as the end of probation periods or medical check-ups.

## Step 2: map the process

Before automating, describe the task as it is carried out today. Who triggers it? What information is needed? Which steps are followed? What happens in case of an exception?

This description, even a brief one, often reveals surprises: unnecessary steps, duplicates, information entered twice in two different tools. Sometimes simplifying the process is enough to save time, even before automating. Automating a confused process just produces confusion faster.

Identify the exceptions too: the invoice in a foreign currency, the customer who pays in installments, the urgent request. Decide from the start how they will be handled, usually by sending them to a person.

## Step 3: start small

Choose a single process to begin with, for example extracting supplier invoice details into a spreadsheet. Define a precise scope: one type of document, one department, a few suppliers.

Measure the time spent before automation, then again after a few weeks. This figure will let you judge the real gain and convince teams and management to go further. A first successful project, even a modest one, is worth more than an ambitious project that gets bogged down.

If you are looking for quick wins, three small automations deliver results in almost every company: an automatic acknowledgment for requests sent to your general address, a weekly reminder listing unpaid invoices, and the automatic filing of email attachments into the right shared folder. None of them requires AI, they take a few hours to set up, and they build the team's confidence. Once people see that these simple workflows run reliably, they are far more willing to try AI-based steps such as reading documents or drafting replies. Starting this way also helps you learn the automation tool itself before you trust it with more sensitive processes.

## Step 4: choose your tools

Today there are many automation tools that don't require programming skills. They let you connect your applications to each other (email, spreadsheets, management software, file storage) and add AI steps to read, classify, or write.

To choose, ask yourself a few questions:

- does the tool connect to the software you already use?
- where is data processed and stored, and is it used to train models?
- does the cost depend on the number of runs, and what volume do you expect?
- who in the company will be able to modify the automation if a need changes?

Avoid multiplying tools. One or two well-mastered platforms are better than a dozen services connected at random.

## Step 5: keep human validation

At first, systematically review the results. AI can misread a figure, confuse two suppliers, or misclassify a request. These errors are all the more problematic because they go unnoticed in an automatic process.

A good practice is to match the level of control to the risk:

- for a low-stakes task, such as sorting emails, spot checks are enough;
- for a task with financial stakes, such as entering invoices, validate every line at first, then reduce checks once reliability is proven;
- for anything sent externally, such as a customer reminder, review the first messages before automating the sending.

Many tools also let you set a confidence threshold: if the AI isn't sure of its reading, the task is sent to a person rather than processed automatically.

## Step 6: document how it works

An automation nobody understands becomes a risk. The day it breaks, or when the person who built it leaves the company, nobody knows how it works anymore.

For each automation, simply record:

- its purpose and the department concerned;
- the connected tools and the accounts used;
- the steps followed and the rules applied;
- the person responsible and what to do when something goes wrong.

Preferably use dedicated service accounts rather than employees' personal accounts. And set up an alert in case of failure, so a stopped automation doesn't go unnoticed for weeks.

## Step 7: secure the automations

Automations often hold the keys to several systems at once: the shared mailbox, the accounting software, the file storage, sometimes the bank portal. That makes them attractive targets and potential sources of accidents.

A few simple rules reduce the risk considerably:

- give each automation only the permissions it needs, for example read-only access to a folder rather than full access to the whole drive;
- store passwords and access keys in the tool's secure settings, never in a shared document or spreadsheet;
- enable strong authentication on the accounts used by the automation platform;
- review the list of active automations every few months and switch off those that are no longer used;
- keep a log of what each automation did, so you can trace an error back to its source.

These precautions take little time to put in place and prevent the most common incidents, such as an automation sending the wrong document to the wrong customer, or an old workflow continuing to run after a process has changed.

## Bringing teams along

Automation sometimes raises concern: will it replace people? Will it make mistakes that someone will be blamed for? These questions deserve honest answers.

Explain that the goal is to remove tedious tasks, not jobs, and show concretely what the freed-up time will be used for: better customer follow-up, faster handling of disputes, more time for training or for projects that keep being postponed. Involve the people who currently do the task in designing the automation. They know the exceptions, the traps, and the shortcuts better than anyone, and their involvement makes adoption much easier.

Recognize their contribution when the automation works. The person who helped map the process and who checks the results has become an expert in that workflow, and that expertise is valuable to the company.

## Keeping automations healthy over time

An automation that works today may break tomorrow. A supplier changes its invoice layout, a software update renames a field, a new tax rate appears. Plan for maintenance from the start.

Set a simple routine: a quick monthly check of error rates and alerts, and a yearly review of each automation's usefulness. Ask whether the process has changed, whether the volume still justifies the tool, and whether a simpler solution now exists. Treat automations like any other piece of equipment: they need occasional servicing to keep delivering value.

## A practical example

An IT equipment distributor in Marrakech receives several hundred supplier invoices every month, by email, as PDFs. An administrative assistant spends a large part of their time opening them, copying the details into a spreadsheet, and filing them.

The company sets up a simple automation. Invoices received at a dedicated address are automatically saved to a shared folder. An AI tool extracts the supplier, date, amount before tax, VAT, and invoice number, then adds a row to the tracking spreadsheet. Rows the tool is unsure about are highlighted in color.

During the first month, the assistant checks every row and corrects a few errors, mostly on poorly scanned invoices. The following month, they only check highlighted rows and a sample of the others. The time freed up lets them follow up on supplier disputes and prepare payments earlier, which improves relationships with partners.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Automating a poorly defined process.** Simplify first, then automate.
- **Removing all checks too early.** Trust is built on measured results.
- **Forgetting exceptions.** Always provide a way out to a person.
- **Depending on a single person.** Document the automation and train at least two people.
- **Neglecting security.** An automation often has access to sensitive data and several tools: protect the accounts it uses and limit their permissions.

## Measuring the return on investment

To evaluate an automation, compare:

1. the time saved each month, multiplied by the hourly cost of the people involved;
2. the reduction in errors and their consequences (delays, penalties, dissatisfaction);
3. the costs: subscriptions, setup time, and time spent on checks and maintenance.

Don't forget the less visible benefits: employees less worn down by repetitive work, shorter turnaround times for customers, better traceability. These effects matter, even if they are harder to quantify.

## In short

AI-powered automation lets SMEs free up valuable time, provided they proceed methodically: spot frequent, stable tasks, describe the process, start small, choose suitable tools, keep human oversight, and document how everything works. The goal isn't to replace teams, but to let them put their energy into work that requires thinking, customer relationships, and expertise.
